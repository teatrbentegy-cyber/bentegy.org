What Will We Tell the World Now That It Has Heard About Our War

Ukrainian culture can’t remain only a culture of testimony. We need not only sympathy, but recognition.

Pavlo Koval

Author
Pavlo Koval
Date
19 August 2026
Reading
14 min read

It was the first shared lunch of a residency in Slovakia. Around the table: the local community that has been helping Ukrainians and displaced people since 2022, Slovaks, and Ukrainians who moved here ten, fifteen, eighteen years ago, as well as the multidisciplinary artists invited to this year’s programme, among them relocated Ukrainians.

We were introducing ourselves. When it was my turn and the word “Ukraine” came up, the conversation, of course, turned that way: to me and to the context I had come with.

And then someone asked:

“Tell me, is all of it really true, what they’re saying is happening in Kyiv right now?”

It wasn’t a Slovak who asked. It was a Ukrainian woman who had left a very long time ago.

It frightened me.

Not because the question was stupid. It was sincere.

And not because she had nowhere to find out. The channels exist. There are media, there are correspondents, there is a feed in which every Ukrainian reposts every strike. More has been published about us in these four years than in all the previous decades put together.

That was exactly what frightened me. She had everything. And she still asked whether it was true.

The Genre We Are Allowed to Exist In

For a long time I carried around the thesis that quality representation of Ukraine abroad barely exists. That isn’t true, and it’s better to admit it straight away than to let someone disprove me in the first comment.

It exists. It just lost on volume of attention and won on genre.

You arrive as an artist and you are taken as testimony. The interviewer’s question starts not with “how did you make this” but with “who have you lost”. Lia Dostlieva described related things earlier and more precisely than I can: Ukrainian cultural workers in the West came to be seen as carriers of trauma, and their work came to be described exclusively in terms of identity. And separately: the public platforms given to Ukrainian culture in the West often turn into a kind of “ghetto”, attended by Ukrainians themselves and a few like-minded people.

And this genre has a shelf life built into it from the start. Global search interest in the word “Ukraine” in February 2022 stood at one hundred points, the highest ever recorded. Throughout 2024 it held at seven. It didn’t fall in some dramatic month. It simply stood at seven all year, the way a background level does.

This is what a register that never changes does. When everything that reaches you about a country is at maximum, people at a distance stop telling the differences within it. First they sympathise. Then they get used to it. Then they start to suspect exaggeration. The question “is it true?” isn’t about the facts. It’s about trust in the form in which the facts arrive.

And there is a second thing that news cannot do in principle. It says what happened. It doesn’t say whether life is possible there. Everyone knows how many drones there were last night. Whether people go to the theatre the next day, nobody knows unless they have asked a living person.

The most precise illustration isn’t ours but Venetian. 2024, Adriano Pedrosa’s biennale “Foreigners Everywhere”, an event that deliberately turned towards those the centre had noticed least: migrants, Indigenous peoples, outsiders, the Global South. Everyone was brought in.

Ukraine was there. Its own pavilion in the Arsenale, a PinchukArtCentre exhibition in the official collateral programme, the Ukrainian Open Group in the Polish pavilion, at the invitation of the Poles.

And in Pedrosa’s exhibition itself, not a single artist from Ukraine among more than three hundred and thirty participants.

We were at the biennale. We were even in someone else’s pavilion. We weren’t in the conversation the biennale was having. Not because we were forgotten. Because we didn’t belong to that conversation: we were a subject, not a voice.

Should We Talk About the War

Yes. That isn’t even a question.

It would be strange to demand that a Ukrainian artist in 2026 stop talking about the war when their country suffers war crimes every day. Testimony is work, and someone has to do it. I do it myself.

The question is a different one.

Should we talk only about the war?

And here I have to be honest: this pressure doesn’t come only from outside. It comes from inside too. We also have an unspoken expectation about which material is appropriate right now and which is a luxury. When we select projects, when we write applications, when we decide what to bring to an audience, we narrow ourselves too, before a foreign curator does it for us.

And now the most unpleasant part.

If Ukrainian culture remains a culture of testimony for ever, Ukraine will remain an object of sympathy for ever.

And sympathy is an emotion that runs out. It is built so that it needs a stronger stimulus every time, and a crisis that goes on becomes invisible precisely because it goes on. And when a European spectator cries at our show, that may be the end of the relationship rather than its beginning: they felt something, they confirmed they are a good person, they are free to go.

Those seven points of search interest are the price of the genre we are allowed to exist in.

What I Caught Myself On

The first thoughts about this began to creep in back in Germany, at ITI Academy Week, in Chemnitz and Berlin, as part of the Theater der Welt festival. But I was so taken with my first international experience that I wanted to write not about this but about the work I saw: about the festival, about the stage, about everything I had finally seen with my own eyes.

It caught up with me here, in Slovakia. In conversations with locals: about corrupt government, about environmental problems, about people who are literally fighting to protect nature. About Gánovce, about Poprad. About how the country separated from Czechoslovakia. About the socialist past, which here is still a living subject and not a paragraph in a textbook.

And for the first time I honestly stopped myself and asked:

why did I never know anything about this?

Not “why did nobody tell me”. Why did I never ask.

I came to a country with a ready intention to talk about myself. I had my theses with me, I had my pain with me, I had the context to immerse them in. I didn’t have a single question for them.

And it isn’t only my personal blind spot. Erasmus+, data from the programme’s National Office: across five calls, from 2021 to 2025, ten thousand and seven mobilities of students and staff from Ukraine to the EU were approved for funding, and one thousand six hundred and seventy-four in the opposite direction. In the youth strand, seven thousand seven hundred and seventy-seven approved mobilities from Ukraine and one hundred and nineteen to Ukraine.

Six to one. And sixty-five to one.

The exchange is physically paved in one direction. We systematically travel to look at Europe. Europe comes to look at us far less often.

Yes, part of this asymmetry is explained by the war: insurers, security regulations, martial law. But the war doesn’t explain what we were doing in the years before it. And it certainly doesn’t explain why I didn’t have a single question at that lunch table.

I don’t think we are too lazy to be curious about others. I think we were never put in conditions where it was necessary. And we didn’t notice how a circumstance turned into a habit.

What Culture Can Do That Diplomacy Can’t

Political diplomacy says: here is our position.

Culture can say something else: this is how we see the world, now show me how you see it.

It is a fundamentally different kind of relationship, and it works even when, especially when, it doesn’t call itself diplomacy. That is why I don’t want to write about cultural diplomacy as a field. What interests me is why culture is diplomacy even when it isn’t doing diplomacy.

Here is the question I now put to every application I submit to festivals, programmes and residencies abroad.

I haven’t yet taken a single one of my shows abroad. For now I am the one applying. But this is exactly the stage at which it is decided what you will eventually bring there: the application is written for what you think they want to hear from you.

What can we offer another society that doesn’t require it to become Ukrainian first in order to understand us?

This isn’t about removing the war. It’s about what we bring with it. Because the war opens questions that aren’t only ours. What is home when you can’t go back to it. What is memory when it is being deliberately erased. What makes a society resilient. What happens to a landscape when people disappear from it. How a person lives after a catastrophe that hasn’t ended yet. What the future looks like after the future has been cancelled.

None of these questions is Ukrainian property. All of them now stand before Europe too, just in other wordings and at a different speed.

And then Ukraine stops being an answer that has to be studied. It becomes a way of asking a question.

Not “look how much it hurts us”.

But “look whether our story holds a question that has long been hurting you”.

Not sympathy, but recognition.

The Strongest Argument Against Everything I Have Written

I’ll make it myself, because otherwise the first commenter will make it and I’ll look naive.

A country that is shelled every night owes nobody symmetrical curiosity. Attention is a resource, and a person who sleeps in a corridor doesn’t have it. The demand that we first study someone else’s context and only then ask about ours is the same entrance exam that is always set for the periphery and never for the centre.

I agree with this. Completely.

And I still think it has to be done. Not because we owe it to them. Because a monologue doesn’t scale.

Have There Been Attempts?

There have. And it matters to say so, because otherwise the text reads as a reproach to people who work harder than I do.

Magda Bizarro, co-director of the artistic programme of the Avignon Festival, who spent five days in Kyiv this spring, singled out the Theatre of Veterans’ Aeneid, directed by Olha Semoshkina, for the way the team works with disability: in her words, it has “a distinct political dimension”, because the subject isn’t pushed to the margins but placed at the centre of the show. Notice what exactly she noticed it for. Not for the war. For a political statement that concerns the European spectator too.

At the festival itself this summer there was “Amadoka”, a Ukrainian-French theatre diptych based on Sofia Andrukhovych’s novel, directed by Jules Audry. And, for the first time, a Ukrainian in Transmission Impossible, the festival’s live laboratory: the actress Yelyzaveta Nykytiuk.

The producer Olha Diatel put the condition of presence simply: “Being present on platforms this important is about regularity and the amount of effort.” Not a breakthrough event. Regularity.

And the Kyiv Biennial made a sharper move. The sixth biennial was spread across Warsaw, Antwerp, Linz, Vienna and venues in Dnipro and Kyiv, and this summer a Berlin chapter is running as well. Its Warsaw exhibition was called “Near East, Far West” and proposed the term Middle-East-Europe: a single space in which Central and Eastern Europe, post-Soviet Central Asia and the Middle East stop being separate outskirts of other people’s centres. The title, according to the curators, points to the current geopolitical reality and demands a reorientation of the very notions of “East” and “West”.

Ukrainians did this. Not the West for us, but us for ourselves.

Volodymyr Yermolenko puts the same thing from another side: to survive, “and this is a great paradox”, Ukrainian culture has to become universal, able to speak not only about itself. I don’t argue with him, but I would add one thing. Universality through translation is when we explain ourselves in more understandable words. What I am talking about is universality through entry: when we enter someone else’s context the same way we want others to enter ours.

So there are attempts. But for now they rest on specific people and specific decisions, not on a rule that will work without them.

The Second Question

Here, at this same residency, I lead performative practice with a local theatre studio, and I work there with displaced children from Ukraine. Today we talked about home: what they remember, what is different here from there.

I asked whether they would like to go back.

One boy answered: if you could go back to pre-war Ukraine, yes. To today’s, no. We’re displaced people. Nobody needs us there any more. While they were fighting, we were here. Nobody in Ukraine will welcome us now.

He is ten years old. He has lived in Slovakia for four of those years.

I don’t know exactly where he got this from. But it sounded as if he had already absorbed some adult’s opinion about people like him.

And this is what I have from a single day. An adult who left many years ago doesn’t know what is happening in Kyiv now. A ten-year-old child who left four years ago knows about us exactly what we say about each other in our worst register, and has already drawn a conclusion about his own future from it.

Both channels work. It’s just that our story about ourselves arrives there as noise. And our grievances against each other arrive without any distortion at all.

I almost wrote a sentence in this text about those who “ran off to the good life”. I didn’t write it. But I thought it.

And a ten-year-old boy in Slovakia already knows that we think it.

And here I’m not talking about emigration statistics. I’m talking about the fact that we, as a culture, haven’t said a single word to these people that wasn’t a reproach. We built representation for foreigners. We built a conversation for ourselves, here. For them we built nothing.

Those people aren’t lost. They’re unaddressed.

What I Can Do

I won’t offer a programme. I don’t have one, and any I made up now would be a lie: I am a twenty-one-year-old director from an independent theatre, not an institution.

But I know what it costs not to be an object. I have already counted that cost in a budget.

International partnerships have an unspoken norm: artists from poorer countries are paid less, because local costs there are lower. This is considered reasonable. In the project we are now putting together with a London company, the fee is the same for everyone, regardless of country, so that nobody in that room is cheaper than anyone else. It costs money, it shows in the budget, and it is the one line that can’t be faked with words about equality.

The same document states that what we develop comes back to Kyiv rather than staying in London.

This isn’t heroism. It’s simply what you have to write into a contract when you don’t want to be a guest in your own project.

I am finishing this at a residency in the High Tatras. The project I came here with is built from the testimonies of Ukrainians displaced by the war, about the landscapes they have lost. And the mountain where I am sitting right now is disappearing in its turn, and the locals are fighting for it.

The easiest thing now would be to turn their mountain into a metaphor for our loss. It works. It’s even beautiful.

I won’t do it.

Two disappearances. One act of listening. Not our story told through their material, but two materials laid side by side. I don’t spell them out. I simply lay them next to each other. That is enough.

That is all I have for now. It isn’t much.

But it is no longer a monologue.
